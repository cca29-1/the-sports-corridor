Why does the force plate read 0 N during the flight phase, even though gravity is still acting on the athlete?

The plate measures force at the contact surface. It does not measure the gravitational force directly.

Understanding free body diagrams

A free body diagram isolates an object and shows all external forces acting on it. The directions of the arrows show force direction. Use labelled arrows and choose a consistent system (e.g. the athlete as a whole).

Newton's laws

First law: If the resultant force is zero, velocity stays constant; the object may be at rest or moving at constant velocity.

Second law: Resultant force = mass × acceleration, ΣF = ma. Acceleration is in the direction of the resultant force.

Third law: When the athlete pushes down on the ground, the ground exerts an equal and opposite force on the athlete. These two forces act on different objects.

Common misconception: Weight and normal reaction may balance, but are not the Newton's third-law pair. The reaction to the ground pushing up on the athlete is the athlete pushing down on the ground.

What does a force plate measure?

During ground contact, a force plate measures the force exerted at its surface, typically resolved into components. This lab focuses on vertical ground reaction force (vGRF).

While the athlete is in contact with the plate:

Fnet, vertical = FGRF − mg

When force exceeds body weight, net vertical acceleration is upwards. When force is below body weight, net vertical acceleration is downwards. During flight, the plate reads 0 N, but weight still acts.

Impulse and jump height

Net impulse during take-off equals the change in vertical momentum, so J = Δ(mv). The area between the vGRF curve and the body-weight line, taken with positive and negative regions, gives net impulse while in contact.

For a purely vertical jump with equal take-off and landing heights, ideal flight time is 2v/g and jump height is v²/(2g). These calculations are approximations, not direct measurements.

This model assumes a rigid body, a vertical jump, constant mass and gravity (9.81 m/s²), and no air resistance.

Exam practice · Forces

Question 1 (4 marks, practice): A 75 kg athlete is standing still. Calculate their weight and explain why they do not accelerate vertically.

Show hint and indicative answer

Weight = mg = 75 × 9.81 = 735.75 N (about 736 N). The upward normal reaction balances the downward weight, so the resultant vertical force is 0 N. From Newton's second law, vertical acceleration is zero.

Question 2 (4 marks, practice): A player accelerates forwards. Draw and label a free body diagram showing the main external forces and explain the acceleration.

Show hint and indicative answer

Show weight downwards, normal reaction upwards, forward horizontal ground reaction/friction from the surface, and opposing air resistance if included. The forward force is greater than resistance. A positive resultant forward force causes forward acceleration (F = ma).

Exam practice · Force plates

Question 3 (4 marks, practice): Explain how a force plate trace can be used to identify take-off and flight during a countermovement jump.

Show hint and indicative answer

At take-off, vertical ground reaction force drops to zero as contact ends. It stays at zero during flight because the athlete does not contact the force plate. The athlete still experiences gravity. The trace rises sharply when contact is regained at landing.

Question 4 (6 marks, practice): Explain how net vertical impulse influences take-off velocity and jump height.

Show hint and indicative answer

Net force is vGRF minus body weight during contact; integrating over the period gives net impulse, equivalent to change in vertical momentum. For an athlete starting from rest, a greater positive net impulse generally means a greater take-off velocity at a fixed mass. Under ideal ballistic assumptions, greater take-off velocity gives greater jump height (v²/2g). Distinguish a high peak force from high impulse, which depends on both force and time.
