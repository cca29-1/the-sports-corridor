Fitts & Posner: how learning develops

01 / COGNITIVEUnderstanding the skill

Movements can be inconsistent. The learner consciously processes instructions, makes frequent errors and needs clear demonstrations and feedback.

Example: a new footballer watches the ball closely while attempting a basic pass.
02 / ASSOCIATIVERefining the skill

Practice strengthens movement patterns. Fewer errors occur, feedback becomes more specific and learners start detecting and correcting their own mistakes.

Example: the player increasingly scans for team-mates while controlling the ball.
03 / AUTONOMOUSPerforming fluently

Well-practised components demand less conscious attention. The performer may devote more resources to tactics, anticipation and complex situations.

Example: an experienced player controls and passes while monitoring available space.
The model describes learning of a particular skill, not permanent athlete labels. Eye movements cannot independently identify a person's learning stage. Performers may be at different stages for different skills.

Visual search strategies

Fixation: a period when gaze remains relatively stable on a location. Saccade: a rapid eye movement between fixations. Selective attention: prioritising relevant cues while filtering out distractions.

Anticipation: using available information to predict what is likely to happen next. Expert performers may develop more efficient cue use, but optimal gaze patterns vary by sport, task and athlete.

Quiet eye

The quiet eye is commonly defined as a final fixation or tracking gaze on a relevant target before a critical movement. It has been studied in aiming and interceptive tasks; it is not a universal measure of skill and should not be equated with simply staring longer.

Think: Which cues are relevant? When should attention switch? How might practice improve the link between perception and action?
Stages of learning — original practice question (6 marks)

A young footballer initially focuses on the ball while passing. After several weeks of structured practice, they begin scanning for team-mates and available space. Explain how the learner may progress from the cognitive to the associative stage, including the role of feedback and selective attention.

Cognitive: new learner consciously controls the movement, makes frequent errors, uses demonstrations and frequent external feedback. Associative: technique becomes more consistent, errors reduce, the learner increasingly detects mistakes and benefits from more specific feedback. Attention can shift towards other relevant cues such as team-mates, defenders and available space. Apply each point to the described passer. This is indicative guidance, not an official OCR mark scheme.
OCR A Level PE — original practice question

Explain how selective attention and anticipation can influence decision-making in an open skill such as passing in football. (4 marks)

Indicative points: selective attention focuses processing on relevant stimuli; irrelevant cues are ignored; anticipation uses prior experience and early cues to predict opponents’ or teammates’ movements; this can allow a quicker or more appropriate pass. Credit well-developed, accurate application. This is an original teaching question, not an official OCR mark scheme.
